Krator: A Primer Sep 19, 2026 · @Travis O'Rear The world Krator is a great crater on a moon, and almost everything about life there follows from two facts: the moon's air is thick at the bottom and thin at the top, and the crater is deep enough to hold a whole world's worth of both. The moon itself is a bit smaller than Earth, with about three-quarters of Earth's gravity, and it orbits a gas giant the way our Moon orbits us: one face always turned toward the planet. It is a cold, dry world overall — most of its surface is thin-aired highland where nothing much grows. But wherever the ground sinks low, the air piles up. In the deep places it is nearly twice as thick as Earth's at sea level, warm, humid, and rich in oxygen: the moon's air is 28 percent oxygen, so the abyss carries nearly three times the oxygen of Earth's sea level. Flight is easy there. Insects grow to the size of birds, the birds grow to the size of gliders, and the largest land arthropods reach the mass of a horse. That much oxygen burns human lungs. The original colonists were altered to bear it; their abyss-born descendants breathe it freely, while outsiders cough and sicken for months before their bodies learn. Where the ground rises, the air thins, the cold bites, and the great flyers cannot follow. Krator is the largest of these low places: an ancient impact basin about 2,200 km across, more than 1,300 miles. Its outer rim is a ring of mountains 8 to 17 km high, so tall that the air runs out on their summits; they are red rock and ice, and nothing crosses them. Inside, the land steps down in stages — a frozen band, then high shelves of cold plateau, then broad lowlands at roughly Earth-like pressure, and finally, at the centre, an abyss around a ring-shaped sea where the air is thickest of all. A single volcano rises out of that sea, and its summit is the highest point anyone can reach. More than a thousand years ago a ship of an advanced human civilisation came down here — a crash or a failed colony, no one now knows which — and its people chose this basin because its floor held the most Earth-like country on the moon. Their descendants have long since fallen to an early-modern level of craft. The relics of the ancients survive as things most people call magic. Sky and seasons The gas giant never moves. Krator lies about 40 degrees south of the moon's equator, so the planet hangs fixed roughly halfway up the northern sky, fifty times the width of a full moon, cycling through its phases once a day. A day is 24 hours, because the moon circles the giant once a day and always shows it the same face. Seasons are Earth-like because the giant is tilted much as Earth is, and the moon shares its tilt. Summer and winter are real, and around each equinox comes a stranger season: for a few weeks the giant passes in front of the sun every day near noon, and the whole basin goes dark for an hour or two under the planet's shadow. The prevailing wind comes from the north-west. At this latitude Krator sits in the belt of westerlies, the same winds that drive the storms of Chile and Patagonia on Earth, and nearly everything about where it rains follows from that one direction. How the weather works Rain in Krator comes from three places: winter storms that ride in over the western rim, the warm lakes and the Ring Sea, and summer thunderstorms over the hot abyss and the high plateaus. The north-west wind carries all of it south-east across the basin, and the land decides where it falls. Air that is pushed up a slope drops its water. Every escarpment that faces north-west is a wet band, and the country just downwind of it, on the far side, is a rain shadow. Because Krator is built of concentric rings, this repeats from the outside in: a wet foot, a dry lee, another wet foot. The sheer faces themselves shed most of what falls on them, so the lushest ground is usually the belt at the base of a cliff, fed by the runoff and springs above. Height sets the temperature. The outer lowlands are warm-temperate; the plateaus 3 km up are cool with hard winters; the frozen band above them is tundra and ice; the rim is airless. Below the lowlands the rule runs the other way: every kilometre down into the abyss adds about 5 degrees, so the Ring Sea sits at 33 °C year-round and the deepest salt basins near 40. Winter is the wet season in the west, where the storms arrive; summer is the wet season in the abyss and on the plateaus, where the heat builds thunderstorms. Places that get neither are steppe and desert. The Centre: the Volcano and the Ring Sea The heart of Krator is an abyss. The whole inner lowland sags a kilometre and a half toward the Ring Sea, a warm ring-shaped sea whose surface lies below the level of the outer lowlands, under air nearly twice as thick as Earth's and at a steady 33 °C. Out of its middle rises the Volcano, a shield 12 km high from shore to summit, its flanks ash-grey and gullied, an ice cap above six and a half kilometres, and at the top a crater rim at 10.9 km with a floor of glowing lava 600 m below it and a plume of smoke that leans away south-east on the wind. A broken arc of islets marks a drowned inner ring, and a field of small cones, geysers and hot springs steams on the shore. The sea's south-eastern shore, where the wind comes off the water, is the hypertropic jungle: a canopy that shimmers green and violet, the densest air and the biggest flyers in the world. Its north-western shore, upwind, is abyssal savanna, and the ring of lowland beyond that, in the shadow of the Inner Wall, is hot desert. The volcano's own lee is a dry streak reaching out across the water. Nights here bring fog seas and pools of heavy gas in the hollows. The North-West The north-west is the settled heart of human Krator and the country the ancients chose. Between the Outer Wall and the Inner Wall lies a broad lowland of long lakes and forest with a Mediterranean climate: warm dry summers, and winters of rain when the storms come over the rim, a thousand millimetres a year on the floor and far more at the cliff feet. It is redwood-coast country, home to the great megaflora and the largest polities. Every north-west-facing escarpment above it is a ribbon of waterfalls with a lush belt at its base, and the Inner Wall's outer face is the wettest ground outside the abyss. Above the lowland, on the western shelves, the climate turns continental: cold plateau winters, warm summers, short grass and dark forest, and a high basin at 3.6 km holding a great mountain lake that spills east through a gorge of cataracts. These uplands are the hill peoples' country, whom the lowlanders call savages and have learned to fear when they come down. The North-East: Korona Korona is a wound that opened after the crater was already there. Long after the impact, a rising plume of hot rock pushed up the floor here and then drained away, and the land above it sank back down a circle 400 km across on ring after ring of faults. What is left is a terraced bowl: concentric ridges that wander, pinch and break, each with a scarp facing inward, stepping down 2.4 km to a floor that is abyssal at the centre. The old plateau rings sank with everything else and now stand as ring-mountains half a kilometre to two kilometres high with hot, thick-aired troughs between them. At the bottom lies the Korona Sea, a closed, saline, pink lake with no outlet. No two rings share a climate. Each north-west face catches rain and grows forest, each lee bench is steppe, and the troughs hold pockets of rainforest and jungle beside stretches of hot desert. Beyond the bowl, the north-eastern lowlands are humid and warm, the mildest ordinary country in Krator. The South-West The south-west is the in-between country. The Ring Sea sends a long southern arm this way, the Sea of Voth, and the land around it grades from the abyssal shore up through savanna and scrub to lightly wooded hills where the north-west lowland's forests give out. Two features shape it. At the bottom of the basin the Outer Wall bends inward in a great notch, the one place where the rim's foothills reach the crater floor, and the passes here are the only road anyone tells stories about crossing. And along the south, between the salt country to the east and the rim, a single broad plateau tilts up from a few hundred metres to nearly 3.5 km against the Outer Wall. Because it rises steadily, it takes rain along its whole width instead of at one cliff: a semi-fertile shelf of wooded valleys and grassland, humid and mild low down, Mediterranean in its middle, cold and continental at the top. The South-East The south-east is Krator's dry quarter, and the strangest of its landscapes. Everything that reaches it has already crossed the Inner Wall, so the lowlands beyond are hot steppe and desert on red soil, the country of the nomads. Along the eastern side runs a chain of deep basins, 2.4 km below the outer floor, where the air is thickest and hottest in the world — around 36 to 40 °C on average, past 50 on a summer afternoon — and too dry ever to fill. These are the abyssal deserts: white salt pans around lakes coloured pink, orange and teal by the algae that live in them, oases only for what can stand the heat. The one exception to the dryness is the shore of the Ring Sea itself, where air coming off the warm water rains on the land: a strip of tropical rainforest and hypertropic jungle, the wettest and most crowded ground in the whole quarter. East of the salt basins the land climbs out of the trough on a tall escarpment and then tilts gently up to the Outer Wall as a second semi-fertile plateau, humid and mild across most of its width, with steppe on its lower stretches. Beyond that, red rock, ice and no air.
