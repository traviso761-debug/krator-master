Every people of the crater speaks a descendant of one English, worn down over the thousand years since the Ancients fell. Tzintzuntzan · the script of the Realm of the Son of the Sun

Vothic Script

Thirty-three letters, one for each sound of the tongue spoken from Shim to Zey'danin. The bowls and loops come from a Georgian hand, the blades and hooked serifs from Daedric. Vowels are round. Consonants end in a cut. A gold thorn marks a breathed sound.

Scribe

Digraphs are single letters: tzh chh tz ch ph th kh sh hl dh ng rh. A doubled vowel is a long one and is written twice. An apostrophe is the syllable cut; a full stop is the diamond. Latin c f j q w x have no letter.

The thirty-three

In the order of the English letters they descend from, as the Daedric list keeps the Latin order. Each letter is named for a word that begins with it, and every name is an English word worn down by the sound changes below. Click a letter to write it in the scribe.

Gold strokes are the added marks: the thorn (breath), the bar, the root (tail).

Marks

Most letters are their own shapes, as in Georgian. Three marks are systematic, so a reader who knows the plain letter can read its marked partner.

The thorn (Thon) is the breath mark. It turns a stop into its aspirated pair, as Purépecha pairs p/ph, t/th, k/kh, ts/tsh, ch/chh. It also sits on hl, the breathed l of Dunmer names like Hlaalu.

The bar crosses the ascender of d to make dh, much as Old English crossed its d to write ð.

The root is a tail below the line for sounds made at the back or the roof of the mouth: ng from n, retroflex rh from the flap r.

From English

The tongue descends from English across a long dark age, worn into a sound inventory between Purépecha and Dunmer: aspirated stops and affricates, a high central vowel, a retroflex r, a breathed l, and a fondness for v and dh. These are the regular changes. The letter names follow them.

Empar de Son · the Empire of the Sun

Izani Script

The capitals of the Empire of Iziz: twenty-seven letters cut in straight strokes, the way they were first carved into Ancient plate and orange ashlar. The tongue is English worn smooth over eight centuries, so it now sounds half Latin and half Outer Rim. Its script is older than the Vothic script and is that script's parent: the Chichani learned to write as the Empire's clients, then bent these letters into brushed loops.

Scribe

Two digraphs are single letters: ch and rr. Words are parted by an interpunct, as on Roman inscriptions; a sentence ends with the sun sign. Latin c w have no letter: w became gu.

The twenty-seven

In English letter order, with ch, ñ and rr filed after their parents, as old Spanish did. Each letter is named for an Izani word that starts with it; every name is an English word run through the changes below. Click a letter to cut it in the scribe.

From English

Where Vothic squeezed English into aspirates and a dark vowel, Izani opened it out. It drops h, adds a vowel after a hard ending, voices consonants between vowels and puts an i before s plus a consonant. That gives it the Romance sound. The Star Wars sound comes from -ar endings, plurals in -z, an x said "sh" and a q for kw.

Sisters, not mother and daughter

Izani and Chichani are separate descendants of English; the Chichani borrowed the Empire's letters, not its speech. The same English word came down two roads. One loan did cross: the Izani people-suffix -ani (from -ian), which the Empire stuck on its southern clients and which they kept as Chichani.

Every Vothic letter descends from an Izani capital, rounded by brush and nib. Most kept their sound. Three things changed on the way:

The sibilants shifted. Chichani speech moved each hiss one step: the Izani letter for s came to write tz, the one for sh (X) came to write s, and the one for zh (J) came to write sh.

Hykkousik · the tongue of the High Coast

Hykkousik Script

The Hykkousoi did not learn letters from the Empire. Their scribes learned them in the drowned towers of Ys, from an Ancient gene-lab's records. Nothing that needed power still worked, so the records that taught them were the ones that never did: letters etched into vial glass, enamelled on tank plates, printed on charts sealed against the sea. Hykkousoi biotech works the same way. It is grown, not wired, from the least electrical people on the Ring Sea. In those records English stood beside the Greek letters of biology, such as σ and ρ factors, λ phage, κ chains and the α-helix. The scribes took the symbols to be letters. So a people who speak worn-down English write it with Σ, Δ, Ω and ψ, and call the letters by the names Ancient biologists used. It was a rival of the lab at Dalab, and some say it gave them their gills.

Scribe

Letters for one sound: th ph kh ps. ē ō are long vowels. ou is written o + y, as in Greek. An initial h is not a letter but a gold breathing mark before the vowel. A full stop is the wave-sun. Latin c is read as k. f q w and a lone u have no letter.

The twenty-six

Twenty-six letters, the number of the English alphabet, which the lab records used for their own text. Each letter keeps the name Ancient biologists called its symbol, worn down by the sound changes below. Under each is the use in the records that taught the Hykkousoi the shape. Twice the scribes took a capital and a small Greek letter for two letters: Δ and δ became d and j, Ν and ν became n and v. Click a letter to write it in the scribe.

From English

Hykkousik sounds Greek: aspirated ph th kh, a front rounded y, long ē ō, the -oi plural and doubled consonants. The English diphthong in high and isle closed to y. That one change gives the people and their city their names:

Hykkousoi is high coast + -oi, the High-Coast folk. Ys is isles.

The Star Wars sound comes from the hard consonants: a lot of k, x and y, doubled stops and the -oi ending. Scyvoi is their name for the nomads, from scythe + -oi, the scythe-folk, for a people who come down to reap after a burn.

Names like Archon, Trigon, Tethys and Amphitriton belong to a learned register. They were taken straight from the Ancient records, much as English took Latin.

Sultanate of Xanadu · the Vale of Erewhon

Xanadui Script

Xanadu has been cut off longer than anywhere else in the crater, and its tongue has drifted furthest. It is still English underneath, but it sounds like Nepali, Tibetan or Persian. It has breathy and aspirated stops, retroflex t and d, a uvular q and nasal vowels. Its grammar has turned round: verbs come last, prepositions have become suffixes, and every statement says how the speaker knows it.

The Latin alphabet was lost. The temple grammarians built a new script from first principles: one letter per syllable, laid out on a grid of how sounds are made, carved round the drums of the prayer wheels. It has the heavy, slanted weight of Tocharian.

Scribe

Each block is one syllable: a consonant carries an a unless a vowel sign changes it. Consonants with no vowel between them stack, the second one hung small beneath the first. A word that ends in a consonant takes the kill stroke. ã ĩ ũ or ṃ add the nasal dot. A full stop is the eight-spoked wheel. Typing shortcuts: sh = ś, ny = ñ, aa ii uu = long vowels.

The sound grid

Thirty-three consonants, laid out as the grammarians drew them: rows by where the mouth closes, columns by how. Two strokes are systematic. The tail curls under a letter to make it breathed: it turns k into kh, and g into the breathy gh. The horn on the head turns a dental into a retroflex. That is how English t and d are written, since they became ṭ ḍ. Click a letter to add it to the scribe.

Vowels

Five vowels stand alone at the start of a word. Everywhere else a vowel is a sign hung on its consonant. Here they are on ka.

Grammar

Isolation let the grammar turn round. The verb goes last. English prepositions slid behind their nouns and fused into case endings. By became the mark of who did a deed, as in Nepali and Tibetan.

Most of all, a Xanadui statement must say how its speaker knows it. Did they see it (-sin, from seen), or do they only report it (-se, from they say)? The Sultan's heralds always use -sin. Pilgrims' tales use -se.

Ancient names

The Xanadui descend from Theodiga, the Ancient arcology at the valley mouth that harnessed the cascade. Its people went up into the Vale when it failed. The valley's names are older than they are. The Ancients who built Theodiga named the country above it from a poem:

In Xanadu did Kubla Khan / A stately pleasure-dome decree

It is Coleridge's Kubla Khan. Xanadu, the sacred river Alph and the Caves of Ice are all names from it, which the Ancients gave and the Xanadui kept long after the poem was lost to them. The poem survives over the wall at Oidong, in the Monks of History's copies. The Monks know where the Sultanate's names come from, and the Sultanate does not.

The Ancients built more than names. The Caves of Ice are an Ancient construction, not a natural cave, and something deep inside them keeps them colder than any cave should be. The Pleasure Dome is nothing Ancient at all. It is the Sultans' own, built for their feasts by a decadent line of rulers. The Monks find it apt that it echoes a poem the Sultans cannot read.

Yuni · the Outer Wall · and the Monks of History

Yuni Letters

UNIV. · AGRIC. & MECH. · EST.

Yuni never lost the Latin alphabet. The town grew up inside the shell of an Ancient technical college, an agricultural and mechanical school, and its letters were on every door: room numbers, department signs, lab labels, the gate. Its name is the gate's first word, UNIV., said as written: Yuni.

Two hands keep the letters. Yuni's own is the gate capitals, a stencil copied from campus signage, used for houses, contracts and the Emir's decrees. The Monks of History write the book hand, a rounded script for copying records, with margin marks they borrowed from Xanadu.

Twenty-seven letters

Both hands keep the twenty-six, plus the one English schoolrooms used to recite at the end of the alphabet: &, "and per se and", which gave the ampersand its name. It sits on the gate, in Agric. & Mech., so Yuni counts it as a letter and says it an. Click a letter to add it to the scribe.

Yuni speech

Yuni speech is close to English, worn the way London English wore: th to f and v, -ing to -in, a dropped final r, a stopped t between vowels.

Its words are clipped like the abbreviations on campus signs. The town spells them out in full on its oldest walls and has not said them in full for centuries. Spelling stays conservative while speech moves, as it does in English.

Several Yuni titles are campus words that kept their prestige after their meaning was lost. The Emir comes from Emeritus, the title on the oldest office doors, which the old families now pass among themselves. Land-working valley folk are Aggis, from the college's Aggies. Where the Geomancers come from is your call; a geomatics or geomechanics department would fit.

The Monks of History keep the most careful English in the crater. They read the Ancient record and speak it near enough to be understood by it. Dalab is the lab, and Daranch is the ranch, in the mouths of people who forgot what either word meant. That fused da- is the clearest sign of a split that runs through Dalab like its castes. The priests speak and write the high tongue, which is our English, held in place for centuries because the God only answers to it. Everyone else speaks the low tongue, which drifted.

The God does not understand the low tongue. Only those who can phrase a question in the high tongue can ask it anything, and that is the whole basis of priestly rule.

High and low

The high tongue

  • Spoken by the priest caste, the staff's descendants, and learned by nobles who want a hearing.
  • Grammar, words and spelling are our English. To the low folk it sounds stiff, ancient and endlessly polite.
  • Written in Latin letters. The God's own replies are cut in its fixed-width letters, as they appeared on its screens; that letterform is holy.
  • Its formal style borrows the God's turns of phrase: courteous, hedged and thorough.
“Please consider the harvest carefully, and tell us what we should plant.”a priest at the mound, facing the lab

The low tongue

  • Spoken by peasants, traders and the Giant guards; it is everyone's first language, priests' children included.
  • No verb endings. Time is marked by particles: di (did) for the past, go for the future, a for something under way.
  • The has fused onto nouns as da-. The plural is -dem (them).
  • Seldom written. When it is, spelling is by ear in Latin letters.

The Iron Republic · Roketstad · the highlands of the Inner Wall

Hajlandsk

SERVIZ GIR SIDIZAN · service gives citizen · “Service makes a citizen”: the Republic's motto, over the Hall

The Republic began as the Empire's Third Legion, which mutinied at Roketstad during the Izani civil war. Its officers were Izani. Its ranks were filled with highland auxiliaries, and the Republic kept the rule that made them equals: service brings citizenship, whoever your father was.

So the Republic speaks two layers at once. Underneath is Hajlandsk (from Highlandish), the auxiliaries' highland English, which drifted towards something Norse and Slavic. On top lie Izani words taken whole from the officers for law, rank and the state, as English took French after 1066. The Republic writes both layers in the Izani alphabet, letter for letter.

How the highland English drifted

It drifted Norse and Slavic. W became v, th hardened to t d, short e broke into je, k softened to č before i, and unstressed vowels dropped out, leaving consonant clusters and a syllabic r a Czech would recognise.

That slid behind the noun and fused to it as -et, as Norse hinn did: hall-et “the hall”, huset “the house”, Tinget “the assembly”. The clans have it too, so it is highland, older than the Mutiny.

The Beast Riders · Mav's Refuge · the hyperjungle

Tł'aai

Tł'aai means “of the Claw”, after the riders' sign of three talon slashes, from English claw. For centuries the beast-riding tribes held the jungle. They were never united, but no outsider dared take them on, and each tribe spoke its own dialect. They sounded something like Mongol and Apache with a Star Wars edge: ejectives, the lateral ł and tł, nasal vowels, vowel harmony and a guttural kh.

Then the Empire came with its mechs. Under Izani rule the tribes took in a great many Izani words and learned the Izani letters to trade. Since Queen Mav united them and won their freedom, the dialects have been drifting together into one Council speech. It is built on the Quetzal tribe's dialect, as the Quetzal hold the throne, and it keeps the Izani words.

Scribe

Romanized Apache-fashion: ' after a consonant makes it ejective, and on its own it is a glottal stop. ł is the lateral hiss and ą ę į ǫ ų are nasal vowels. kh is written with Izani H, and a long vowel is written twice.

The letters

The tribes took the Izani alphabet as traders, not as subjects, and bent it to their sounds. The Republic changed nothing; the Riders made four additions, shown in red. Izani H, silent in Izani mouths, was given the riders' kh, much as the Chichani gave it a sound.

From English

The changes made the tribes' English sound like the steppe and the desert. Cl became the Apache lateral tł'. Th became the lateral hiss ł. English glottalises a final t, and the tribes made it a true ejective. They also put a vowel before an initial r, as Mongol does (Mongolian Oros for Russia).

Grammar

Verbs that know what they carry

As in Apache, a verb of giving, carrying or dropping says what kind of thing moves. A prefix, worn down from an English noun, classes the object: bii- a beast (from beast), istik- something long like a lance (stick), bol- something round like an egg (ball), shit- something flat like a hide (sheet). Bii-gib is “give a beast” and istik-gib is “give a lance”. A rider never says just “give”.

Endings in harmony

The endings follow the word's vowels, Mongol-fashion: back vowels take back endings and front vowels take front ones. -ad/-ed is “to, at” (from at), -in/-ün is “of”, and -san/-sen marks the past (from done). -nar/-ner pluralizes a people or a tribe. The verb comes last.

The Ash form is worked out from the Zeijani by the brothers' differences. The emphatics soften (ṣ ṭ q to s t k), ü becomes i, an initial w becomes m, and the final -m of the case endings falls away. In wedges, a long vowel is written twice. A vertical wedge parts words, as at Ugarit.

The letters

Twenty-five letters. Most are Latin capitals with their curves cut straight: O, C and Q are gone, and so are F and V, since the tongue lost those sounds. One mark is systematic: the corner wedge, cuneiform's Winkelhaken, turns a plain consonant into its emphatic partner. Š is an S with a wedge struck down through it, from SH run together. Cousins

One change shows the three are kin: English initial s had already become ts on the island. The Chichani kept it as tz. The Zeijani made it emphatic ṣ, and the ash nomads let it relax back to s. After that the roads part. Chichani kept dh where the brothers have z (dha against ze, "the"), and the brothers grew case endings that Chichani never had.

Grammar

Cases, in harmony

Nouns take case endings, and the ending's vowel follows the word's, Turkish-fashion. The subject ends -um after a back vowel and -im after a front one, from English one (“the sun one”). The place where something is takes -in / -un, from in. The object takes -an / -en, from 'em. Peoples take the plural -ī.

Verb last

The verb ends the sentence, and the past adds -d- (from -ed) with a person ending: ḫidd-ū, “they hid”. The ash nomads, like late Babylonian, wore the -m off their case endings. Ṣunum “the sun” is sunu on the plains. That loss is most of what makes the brothers' speech hard work for each other.
