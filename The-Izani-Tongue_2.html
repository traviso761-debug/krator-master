A constructed language · Revision 2

The Izani Tongue

The speech of the Izan — the forest-folk of the city Iziz. Its sounds, its word-building rules, and its growing lexicon, set down so the language grows from one consistent palette instead of a contradictory pile. A soft, forest-dwelling speech.

New in this revision

Everything the city of Iziz now carries on its walls, gathered back into the handbook: a city lexicon of 65 words coined by the rules below, 21 place names and 8 sky names, the naming patterns behind them, larger tally numbers, and every inscription in the city with its script, reading and gloss. New sections are marked new.

One piece of the world is now settled: Iziz is a forest world orbiting a red dwarf.

The rest of the lore and the sky names are placeholders, marked draft, until the geography, history and stellar cartography of Iziz are settled.

How to read the sound charts below

Both charts are a map of your mouth. A consonant is placed by where you make it and how; a vowel by where your tongue sits and how far your jaw drops. Say each one aloud — you can feel the answer.

Consonants · rows — how the air moves

Stop
block the air, then burst it out — feel the pop in t, k
Fricative
squeeze air through a gap so it hisses — s, sh, f, th
Nasal
air out the nose — hum m, pinch your nose, it dies
Liquid
air flows smoothly around the tongue — l, r
Semivowel
barely a consonant, a quick glide — w, y

The paired letters (t d, s z, f v) are the same sound with one difference — your voice box. Say a long ssss, then zzzz, with a hand on your throat: z buzzes. Voiceless sits on the left of each pair, voiced on the right.

…and the vowel chart

Columns — front / central / back
where your tongue sits. Say ee → ah → oo slowly: the sound slides from the front of your mouth to the back.
Rows — high / mid / low
how far your jaw drops. Say ee → eh → ah: your mouth opens wider each step — ee nearly closed (high), ah wide open (low).

The character of the sound

Few stops, many fricatives, and a lot of liquids and nasals — so words flow and hiss rather than clatter. There are no labial stops (p, b) and no guttural kh/gh; that absence is exactly what makes iz, vae and sel sound the way they do. Add hard sounds only if you want a harsher dialect.

Vowels

A vowel is just where your tongue sits, in two directions — so the chart is a map of your mouth. Front ↔ back (the columns): say ee → ah → oo and feel the tongue slide from the front of your mouth to the back. High ↔ low (the rows): say ee → eh → ah and feel your jaw drop lower each step. That places every vowel — i high-front, a low-central, o mid-back. Five pure vowels, plus one gliding diphthong; words lean vowel-heavy.

Diphthong ae — an "ah" gliding into "eh," as in vae (water). Treat it as a single vowel for syllable-counting.

How sounds combine

A tiny set of structural rules keeps every word recognizably Izani. The word-building affixes that ride on top of them come next, in Derivations.

(C)V(C)Syllable shape. Optional consonant, a vowel, optional consonant. iz, sa, sel all fit. No heavy clusters — that’s what keeps it flowing.
X → X-XDoubling = "a mass of." Reduplicate a root to mean a collection of it. iz "tree" → iziz "forest"; sa "house" → sasa "city." This is the rule your city name is built on.
V·V → V-l-VLinking l. When a vowel-final root meets a vowel-suffix, an l slips between so two vowels never collide: sa + -an → salan. Consonant-final roots just take the suffix: iz + -an → izan.

Syntax

You voted "something else," and there’s a clean way to get it: make Izani head-final — the head of a phrase comes last. That one call answers almost everything below, and it isn’t arbitrary: an all-suffix language leans right, so head-final is its natural partner. English is head-initial; going the other way makes Izani feel foreign without becoming random.

S · O · VVerb goes last. Subject, object, then verb: izan izuz izak — "forester tree-obj plants."
mod → nounModifiers precede the noun — adjective, number, demonstrative, and possessor all come before it: izi sa "wooden house," she iz "three tree," izan izir "the forester’s axe."
noun → PPostpositions, not prepositions — the relation-word follows the noun: iziz esh "in the forest," iziz il "to the forest."
-uzOne case: the object. The thing acted on takes -uz; the subject stays bare. That’s the whole case system — place and direction are postpositions. (Kept on purpose — because the object is marked, you can shift word order for emphasis and still tell who did what.)
no pluralNumber, not plural. Nouns never inflect for number. Count with numeral + singular (she iz "three tree"), or use reduplication for a mass (iziz "trees, forest"). Base-10: mi 1 · lo 2 · she 3 · ven 4 · tas 5 · shen 10, built additively (shen lo 12, lo shen 20).
… na?Questions. Yes/no: add na at the end. Wh-words (vi what, ke who, vo where) stay in place — no fronting: izan viuz izak na "the forester plants what?"
un + VNegation. Put un "not" before the verb: izan izuz un izak "the forester does not plant the tree." A negative question just adds na.
[clause] nounRelative clauses come before the noun, like a big adjective, with no "who/which": [my-name-obj stole] man = "the man who stole my name."
[clause]-ki VA clause used as subject or object also sits before the verb, closed by -ki "that": [he liar is]-ki claims = "he claims he’s a liar."
removedWhat Izani leaves out. No articles (a/the) and no grammatical tense — definiteness from context or a demonstrative, time from an optional time-word. Fewer moving parts, and further from English.

Register & expressions

Izani’s register is blunt. It sounds soft, but it doesn’t pad. No word for "please," no polite or formal tiers, no honorifics — a request is just the bare verb (izak! "plant it!"), and a greeting states a fact instead of offering a wish. Short, literal, unhedged.

SayMeaning & literal sense
veshexprhello / goodbye — literally "still standing, hale." One word for meeting and parting.
thaexpryes — a flat affirmative. (Or just echo the verb: even blunter.)
unexprno — the very same word as "not." No softer form exists.
maeexprgood — also carries "thanks." Acknowledgment, not effusion.
un maeexprbad, "no good" — and about as close as Izani gets to an apology.

Bluntness is a pragmatic choice, not a phonetic one — the sound stays soft, only the scaffolding is stripped. Useful later: an over-polite speaker is exactly how you’d mark an outsider, someone who isn’t Izan.

On signs new

The register reaches the walls. A sign is one word — the place, never an instruction: muneth, not "bread sold here." Slogans keep the verbless shape of un mae: thur un mae "war: no good," nesh mae "cats: good." A topic followed by a judgement is the blunt way to hold an opinion.

the logicThe shapes aren’t random. A voiced consonant is its voiceless twin plus one stroke (t→d, s→z, f→v, sh→zh). Nasals stand on a foot at the base; liquids l/r are mirror strokes; the glides y/w fork open at the top. Vowels drop the stem entirely — open angular marks — so they read lighter than consonants, which suits a vowel-heavy language.

The world settled

Iziz is a forest world orbiting a red dwarf. Its light is low and red; the forest covers almost everything, and the city grew up inside it rather than in a clearing cut from it. Old trees stand both inside the walls and throughout the forest beyond them.

Lore draft

Placeholder. These notes were written to give the city’s inscriptions a context. They will change once the geography, history and stellar cartography of Iziz are finalised; treat nothing here as canon yet.

The Izan say their first word was iz, tree, and that every other word grew from it the way branches grow from a stem. So their letters are stems with branches, cut with a knife along the grain. Every child’s first carving is iz.

They do not say the city was built. They say it was planted: izak, not salak. The first house stood beside the first tree, which is why the full name, Izisa, means tree-house. That tree, Izor, still stands on the hill below the Salor; the old trees inside the walls (saliz, noriz, vaeliz, shaeliz) are never cut. Iziz iz is cut over the inside of every gate.

Three gates open on the world: Vaedun faces south towards Vaeren and the lake, Shaedun faces west towards the evening sky, and Seldun faces north-east under the palace hill. The doors drop at night, and the watchmen, halan, are posted (halak) at dawn and dusk.

The walls are as blunt as the speech. A sign is one word, the place: muneth, koreth. There are no signs that ask or warn, because there is no word for please. Paint is for the young: soldiers’ children write thur un mae, and lovers paint two names side by side. Given names often take -is or -or: Shaelis, Izor.

Above the city: Sor, the red dwarf; Nu, the green moon, waxing and waning over eight days; Nuis, the pale moon, crossing every twenty hours. The carvers name five figures: Kalir, Fenis, Ush, Tor and Lolo.

Every third evening is Yolshae, with fenshae over the Shaesa. On feast days the boatmen race (naethur) on Shaevae, the star-water. Its water leaves as Vaeren, and at the falls the carvers wrote vaeren vae: time passes. Trade comes up Naeten, the boat road, from Naeleth to the Water Gate; travellers sleep at Dometh. The moat is Thurvae, war-water.
